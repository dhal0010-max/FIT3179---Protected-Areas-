Executive Overview: The Dual Nature of Australia’s Conservation Estate

How does Australia compare with the global ambition to conserve at least 30% of land, inland waters, coasts and oceans by 2030—and what can a national total hide? The Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework Target 3 sets that ambition, while CAPAD 2024 reveals a clear contrast in the area recorded as protected on land and at sea.

Marine Protection Exceeds 30% by Area

About 437.2 million hectares of marine area are recorded as protected—48.9% of the 8,939,192 km² Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) used in this comparison. That is above 30% by area, but does not tell us how effectively places are managed or whether habitats are well represented.

Terrestrial Protection Remains Below 30%

The state extract records about 173.5 million hectares as protected, or 22.6% of the 768,828,859 ha land baseline. Reaching 30% by area would mean a further 57.2 million hectares—about 7.4 percentage points.

These figures compare area only; they do not measure how well each place is managed or whether all habitat types are represented. The national land baseline and the sum of the state rows differ slightly; details are in the data notes below. To see how that national contrast is distributed, we begin by comparing protected shares across jurisdictions.

Different States, Different Conservation Stories

The maps reveal why “most protected” can mean two different things: the largest share of a jurisdiction's land, or the largest contribution to Australia's protected-land total. The figures below separate those measures.

Highest and Lowest Shares

The ACT has the highest protected share in the state extract at 55.9%, followed by Tasmania at 42.8%; Queensland has the lowest share at 9.4%. A high percentage does not necessarily mean the most hectares.

The Largest Protected Areas

By area, Western Australia leads with about 78.67 million hectares, or 45.3% of the national protected-land total used here. WA, the Northern Territory (33.2 million hectares; 19.1%) and South Australia (30.0 million hectares; 17.3%) together account for about 81.7%.

Who Cares for These Places? Indigenous Leadership and Different Types of Protection

Governance is one part of the story; management purpose is another. These records show how protected areas are grouped by governance and by IUCN category—an international classification of management objectives. Neither classification, by itself, measures management effectiveness.

Indigenous Protected Areas

Indigenous Protected Areas (IPAs) account for about 49.5% of protected land in the governance data (85.86 million hectares), compared with 38.4% (66.51 million hectares) in the government category. These dataset categories cannot capture every form or outcome of caring for Country.

Terrestrial IUCN Categories

On land, the largest area is Category VI, where sustainable use is allowed: 99.94 million hectares (57.6% of the category data). National parks (Category II) account for 39.56 million hectares (22.8%).

Marine IUCN Categories

At sea, Category II (national parks and sanctuaries) covers 181.45 million hectares, while Category IV (habitat and species management areas) covers 122.79 million hectares.

Categories describe stated management objectives, not a score of how effective a reserve is. Their totals differ slightly from the supplied national total.

Protection Across Australia's Ecological Regions

State averages still cannot tell us whether protection is spread across different environments. The regional data offers another lens: it shows 15 Australian ecological regions in this project extract against the historical 17% land benchmark from CBD Aichi Target 11. This is a historical comparator, not a current target for each region.

Higher Coverage in the Extract

In this extract, MacDonnell Ranges (81.3%), Nullarbor (54.9%), Wet Tropics (53.5%) and Great Sandy Desert (49.9%) are above the 17% comparator.

Regions Well Below the Comparator

Mitchell Grass Downs (1.56%), Finke (4.6%), Murchison (7.0%) and Pilbara (8.8%) are below 17%. These differences point to places for further assessment and locally led conservation planning.

What Next? Priorities for 2030

Together, the charts show extensive recorded marine protection, a substantial role for Indigenous Protected Areas, and uneven terrestrial protection across states and ecological regions. These patterns suggest questions for further work—not forecasts or official policy recommendations. The methodology that follows explains the data sources, definitions and limitations behind those comparisons.

  1. Understand Marine Protection: Continue assessing management and habitat representation across the large recorded marine estate, including its major Category II and IV areas.
  2. Explore the Terrestrial Gap: Consider pathways toward the 30% by-area ambition alongside ecological representation, connectivity, and effective management.
  3. Recognise Indigenous Stewardship: Build long-term partnerships and resourcing that support Indigenous Protected Areas and Indigenous-led conservation.
  4. Investigate Regional Gaps: Use results such as Mitchell Grass Downs to guide further assessment and locally led conservation planning.

Ultimately, lasting conservation depends not only on how much land and sea is protected, but also on what is protected, how it is cared for, and whose knowledge guides that work.

Methodology & Data Footnotes

This section follows the same order as the story: first the source data, then how the headline percentages are calculated, followed by geographic scope and known limitations. Figures should be read as area-based comparisons, not as measures of conservation effectiveness.

1. Data Sources and Reference Periods

  • Terrestrial Protected Areas: DCCEEW CAPAD 2024 Terrestrial Summary Tables, Table 1. CAPAD is Australia's national protected-areas database. “2024” is the dataset edition/reference year; consult the DCCEEW download metadata for the table's exact publication date and any field-specific reporting cut-off.
  • Marine Protected Areas: DCCEEW CAPAD Marine 2024 Summary Data Tables, Table 1 and Footnote 4. The tables cover Commonwealth and state waters; Footnote 4 documents the EEZ denominator and external-territory scope.
  • Ecological Region Boundaries: IBRA 7.1, published in 2025 as an update to IBRA 7.0. Chart 11 compares CAPAD protection data against these boundaries.
  • Population: Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), Regional Population, 2023–24 (catalogue 3218.0); the chart's population field is labeled 2024.
  • State Land Area Baselines: The supplied CSV does not identify its source table, publication year, or boundary vintage. ABS Year Book Australia (catalogue 1301.0) and the Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) are relevant references, but the exact source series has not been verified.

2. How the Headline Percentages Are Calculated

  • Terrestrial protection: Protected terrestrial area ÷ 768,828,859 ha × 100. Using the state extract total (173,478,189 ha) gives 22.56%, rounded to 22.6%.
  • State/territory contribution to the NRS: Jurisdiction protected area ÷ 173,493,468 ha × 100.
  • Marine protection: Protected marine area ÷ 8,939,192 km² × 100. The jurisdiction extract totals 437,204,396 ha (4,372,043.96 km²), giving 48.91%, rounded to 48.9%.
  • Terrestrial gap to 30%: The difference between the 22.6% protected share and the 30% comparison benchmark is about 7.4 percentage points, or approximately 57.2 million hectares using the supplied land baseline.

3. Geographic Scope and Area Measures

  • CAPAD hectare figures use the spatial GIS_AREA measure in an Albers Equal Area projection for Australia, rather than gazetted legal boundary definitions. Antarctic territory uses WGS 1984 Antarctic Polar Stereographic.
  • The 8,939,192 km² marine denominator is documented in Footnote 4 of the DCCEEW CAPAD Marine 2024 Summary Data Tables: “The total EEZ area of 8,939,192 km2 is based on the ratification of Perth 97 treaty EEZ. It includes external territories of Norfolk Island, Christmas Island, Cocos (Keeling) Islands and Heard Island and McDonald Islands.” Thus, the comparison includes the mainland EEZ and those four external territories.
  • Geoscience Australia's maritime jurisdiction boundary information is the geographic reference for maritime zones. State coastal waters follow its coastal-water boundaries and exclude tidal estuary components.

4. Data Checks and Limitations

  • Land-area baseline: The project uses 768,828,859 ha as its supplied national baseline. Geoscience Australia reports 768,828,700 ha (7,688,287 km²), a difference of 159 ha.
  • Protected-area totals: The supplied national denominator is 173,493,468 ha. Current state_protection.csv rows sum to 173,478,189 ha (15,279 ha less); iucn_categories.csv sums to 173,482,817 ha (10,651 ha less). The two extracts differ by 4,628 ha. The available files do not establish why, so no overlap, projection, or rounding explanation is assumed. The national denominator is not the sum of current state rows.
  • Spatial processing: The project CSVs do not include enough processing metadata to characterize overlap treatment in every exported summary.
  • Interpretation: Area percentages measure how much area is recorded as protected. They do not measure management effectiveness or habitat representation, and the 17% regional reference is a historical 2020 benchmark, not a current target for each region.
